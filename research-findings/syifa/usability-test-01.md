# Syifa — early concept user testing

## What we showed

Syifa guided one participant through the early watch screens and asked what they understood and expected. The transcript labels this person Participant 3. That label belongs to this testing record and does not identify the interviewer or participant in the separate research interviews.

The introduction explained that the prototype showed the interface only. It could not yet send messages, track another watch, give directions or send SOS. The discussion explored expectations about these features.

## Controls and friend colours

The participant recognised the time, battery and connection count, but was unsure what Back and Next would do. They also needed an explanation of the friend's colour. They preferred a simple menu and wanted to know which friend had disconnected or had a weak connection.

## Finding a friend

The participant paid more attention to the arrow than the edge light. They initially thought they could walk straight towards the friend, then recognised that barriers could be in the way. They suggested wording that makes clear the display gives a direction or distance rather than a route.

They expected the other person to receive a notification when someone was trying to find them, and to be able to refuse. They also suggested vibration to reduce looking at the screen while walking. This was a suggestion, not a tested physical feature.

## Messages and replies

Prepared messages felt useful because typing on a small device would be difficult. The participant expected confirmation before sending and a notification afterwards. They wanted the recipient to be clearer.

“Taking a Break” could mean leaving the venue, resting alone or wanting company. They suggested messages such as “Come join me”, “Coming” or “Let's meet up later” to make the next action clearer.

## SOS and privacy

The participant understood the three OK presses and expected SOS to reach connected friends rather than security or emergency services. They were concerned about accidental use or someone sending an alert as a joke. They suggested a cancellation period and including the sender's name and location for the recipient.

They supported giving permission before being found, even if permission was requested each time. They preferred a touchscreen in the hardware discussion and suggested checking wrist weight and whether notifications would be noticeable.

## What we learnt

- Explain navigation and friend colours before use.
- Make connection status and message recipients easy to understand.
- Give messages enough context and consider simple replies.
- Let people know when someone wants to find them and give them a choice.
- Explain that the arrow gives an approximate direction, not a walking route.
- Make the purpose and cancellation of SOS clear.

These findings describe the participant's interpretation of the concept. They do not establish working Bluetooth tracking, hardware comfort or performance in a crowd.
