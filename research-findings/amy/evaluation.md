# Amy — early concept user testing

## What we showed

Amy guided two participants through the early web screens and festival scenarios. The record labels them P1 and P2. They explained what they thought each screen meant and what they expected to happen next.

This was before the physical bands were ready. Messages, friend-finding and SOS were shown as proposed interactions, rather than working hardware features.

## Controls and friend colours

The participants could understand the main purpose of the watch and the short menu. They wanted to know which friend had disconnected, rather than only seeing the number of connected people.

The controls also raised questions about the final hardware. Participants discussed whether touch, physical buttons or a rotary control would be easier, but did not compare physical devices in this session.

## Finding a friend

The arrow was clearer than the edge light. However, the distance on screen could look more accurate than it really was, and the arrow could be mistaken for a walking route around barriers.

Participants wanted the friend being found to know what was happening and be able to respond. Agreeing who waits or moves would make reconnecting easier than both people continuing to move.

## Messages and replies

Prepared messages felt quicker than typing on a watch. Participants wanted the recipient to be clear before sending and a confirmation afterwards.

“Taking a break” did not always explain what the sender wanted their friends to do. Replies such as “Coming with you” or “Meet later” could help the group agree on a plan.

## SOS and privacy

The participants understood the three OK presses, but raised concerns about speed and ease of use during an emergency. They suggested comparing other confirmation methods and allowing a short cancellation period.

They expected SOS to reach friends and wanted that destination to be clear. They also wanted control over location sharing, including knowing when someone was finding them and being able to decline.

## What we learnt

- Keep prepared messages and the menu short.
- Show who receives a message and whether it arrived.
- Give messages enough context and consider simple replies.
- Let friends agree who waits and who moves.
- Explain that direction and distance are approximate, not a walking route.
- Make location sharing and SOS choices clear.

The session explored how people understood the early interface. It did not test physical tracking, message delivery or hardware comfort.
