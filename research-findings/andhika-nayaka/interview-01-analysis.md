# Interview 01 — Analysis

## Research context

This semi-structured interview explored a concertgoer's experiences with group
coordination, phone use, navigation, loud environments, and optional haptic
features. The participant is hard of hearing in one ear and uses hearing
technology in everyday settings.

## Main findings

- Poor mobile reception is the participant's largest communication problem at events.
- Crowded spaces make movement and physically locating separated friends difficult.
- Loud music makes verbal communication unreliable, particularly when someone is calling from a distance.
- Simple physical signage can be more useful than a confusing event map.
- Music-driven vibration may be useful for some people, but it should be optional and adjustable.
- Staying connected with friends is a higher priority than adding entertainment features.

## Observed behaviours

- Attends concerts and festivals with friends.
- Coordinates through WhatsApp or LINE.
- Uses an agreed meeting point when the group expects to separate.
- Brings a power bank, so battery life is not a major concern.
- Records performances frequently and considers this part of preserving memories.
- May turn hearing technology off during loud concerts because the amplified sound can become too intense.

## Pain points

- Weak reception prevents timely updates when friends separate.
- It is difficult to know whether friends are waiting or have moved elsewhere.
- Dense crowds make movement and visual searching difficult.
- Calling someone's name is ineffective in a loud environment.
- Confusing venue maps add friction to navigation.
- Continuous vibration could become distracting or uncomfortable.

## User concerns

- A design must not assume that every hard-of-hearing person has the same needs.
- Users need control over vibration intensity and a way to disable it.
- Friend-finding must remain useful when normal mobile reception is poor.
- Navigation cues must be simpler than the maps they are intended to replace.

## Key quotes

> “Poor phone reception and crowded spaces are probably the two main issues for me.”

> “If someone is trying to call my name from far away during a concert, I may not hear them.”

> “If I hadn't walked past that entrance or hadn't seen them there, it could have been a very different situation.”

> “Users should be able to adjust [the vibration] like a volume button on devices.”

> “I think improving signals or reception would make it easier to connect to others or access information that we need.”

## Affinity-map themes

| Theme | Codes |
| --- | --- |
| Connection | poor reception, separation, uncertainty, meeting points |
| Accessibility | unilateral hearing difficulty, loud environment, adjustable haptics |
| Navigation | confusing maps, physical signage, simple directions |
| User control | vibration strength, on/off control, individual preference |
| Priority | reliable communication, friend-finding, access to information |

## Design implications

1. Treat group reconnection as the primary problem to solve.
2. Support an offline or low-connectivity interaction model.
3. Communicate through multiple channels—visual and optional haptic—without relying on sound.
4. Make haptic intensity adjustable and include an obvious off setting.
5. Use simple direction or proximity information rather than a detailed map on a small display.
6. Minimise phone interaction while allowing a companion app to handle setup and customisation.

## Opportunity statement

How might we help friends find and update one another in crowded, loud venues
when mobile reception is unreliable, while keeping interactions brief,
accessible, and under the user's control?
