# Amy evaluation

[Download the original Word document](Evaluation_Amy.docx)

## 1. Evaluation objectives

The evaluation explores:

- Whether users understand the purpose of the watch.

- Whether users understand how predefined messages would work.

- Whether the proposed messages provide sufficient social awareness.

- Whether users understand the Find Friend sequence.

- Whether the compass light, arrow and distance display are understandable.

- Whether users understand the intended SOS process.

- Whether the proposed system supports cooperation rather than only supplying information.

- What privacy, safety or social concerns users anticipate.

- Whether users would prefer touchscreen, buttons or rotary interaction for the final physical prototype.

## 2. Method

A scenario-based interface walkthrough and semi-structured interview were used.

Because the social and technical functions are not yet implemented, the facilitator:

- Presented each mock-up screen in sequence

- Explained that the prototype does not currently send real data

- Asked participants to think aloud

- Asked what they believed would happen after each action

- Presented realistic festival scenarios

- Asked participants to describe how they and their friends would respond

- Recorded confusion, expectations, anticipated behaviour and design suggestions

No claims were made about actual Bluetooth reliability, navigation accuracy, offline communication or SOS delivery.

## 3. Proxy participant profiles

### Participant 1 - P1

- Age: 22

- University student

- Regularly attends concerts with friends

- Familiar with smartwatches and navigation applications

- Usually keeps their group together but sometimes separates for food or different performers

### Participant 2 - P2

- Age: 24

- University student

- Occasionally attends concerts and festivals

- Has limited experience with compass-based navigation

- Has previously experienced weak reception at a crowded event

## Transcript 1

### Introduction and consent

Facilitator: Thank you for participating. Today, I will show you a mid-fidelity prototype of a festival watch designed to help friends communicate and find one another.

The prototype currently demonstrates only the interface. It cannot yet send messages, track another watch, provide real directions or send an SOS. I will show you different screens and ask you to explain what you think they mean and what you would expect the completed system to do.

We are testing the design, not your ability. You can stop at any time. With your permission, I will take notes, but your name will not be included. Do you consent to participate?

P1: Yes.

Facilitator: Before we begin, can you tell me about your experience attending concerts or festivals?

P1: I go to concerts a few times a year. Usually, I go with three or four friends. We try to stay together, but people might leave to get food or use the bathroom.

Facilitator: Have you experienced difficulty contacting or finding friends?

P1: Yes. At a large concert, messages sometimes arrived late. We normally choose a meeting point, but people forget or the area becomes too crowded.

### Initial screen

Facilitator: This is the watch’s home screen. Please describe what you notice.

P1: I see the time, the event name, battery level, Bluetooth and “four connected.” There are Back, OK and Next buttons and an SOS button at the bottom.

Facilitator: What does “four connected” mean to you?

P1: Four people’s watches are connected as a group.

Facilitator: Would you assume they are connected through Bluetooth, internet or something else?

P1: Because the Bluetooth symbol is there, I would assume Bluetooth. But I do not know whether everyone has to be close.

Facilitator: What would you expect to happen if one person moved outside the connection range?

P1: I would expect the number to change or the watch to show that one person is disconnected.

Facilitator: The current prototype cannot test that. What information would you need from the final watch?

P1: It should show which person is disconnected, not only change from four to three.

Observation: P1 correctly identified the group-connection information but expected individual connection status and range feedback.

### Understanding the navigation controls

Facilitator: Without pressing anything, what do you think Back, OK and Next do?

P1: Back and Next move through menu options, and OK selects one.

Facilitator: Is anything unclear?

P1: Not really. But the square Next and Back buttons look clickable, while OK is round. That is fine, although I wonder whether the final watch will have physical buttons or a touchscreen.

Facilitator: Would these controls be easy to use at a festival?

P1: Physical buttons might be easier because you can feel them. If they are just touch targets, they might be small.

### Opening the menu

Facilitator: Imagine that pressing Next changes the highlighted option to Find Friend. The prototype now displays this screen. What do you think is happening?

P1: Find Friend is selected. The dots at the bottom suggest there are several options.

Facilitator: How would you open it?

P1: Press OK.

Facilitator: What other menu options would you expect?

P1: Send Message, Find Friend, maybe Group Status, Settings and SOS-although SOS already has its own button.

Facilitator: Would you want many menu options?

P1: No. At a concert, I would only want the important ones.

### Selecting a friend

Facilitator: After selecting Find Friend, the next screen displays “Nayaka,” “Purple” and “OK to Find.” What does this mean?

P1: Nayaka is one person in the group, and purple is their assigned colour. I can press Next or Back to move between people, then OK to find them.

Facilitator: Is the colour useful?

P1: Yes, if each friend has a different colour. The light around the watch could use that colour while finding them.

Facilitator: What do you expect to happen when you press OK?

P1: The watch starts finding Nayaka and shows the direction.

Facilitator: Would Nayaka know that you are trying to find them?

P1: I am not sure from this screen. I think they should receive something.

Facilitator: Why?

P1: If they keep moving, it might be harder to meet. They should get a message saying I am coming to them.

Facilitator: What should they be able to do?

P1: They could press “Wait here” or “Find me too.”

Observation: The friend-selection flow was understandable, but P1 expected reciprocal notification and coordination.

### Direction and distance screen

Facilitator: The next mock-up displays Nayaka’s name, a purple arrow, “24 m” and “Finding.” What do you believe this information means?

P1: Nayaka is approximately 24 metres away, and the purple arrow shows the direction I should walk.

Facilitator: The concept also proposes lighting around the edge of the watch, like a compass. What would you do with that light?

P1: I would turn my body until the light is at the top, then walk in that direction.

Facilitator: Which would you pay more attention to: the edge light or the arrow?

P1: Probably the arrow. The edge light might be useful when turning, but the arrow is clearer.

Facilitator: What does “24 m” communicate?

P1: Direct distance, I assume.

Facilitator: Would you interpret it as a safe walking route?

P1: Maybe at first, but that could be dangerous. There could be a barrier, a stage or a dense crowd between us.

Facilitator: What wording would reduce that misunderstanding?

P1: It could say “approximately 24 m away” and maybe show that it is only a direction, not a route.

Facilitator: Would you continuously look at the watch while walking?

P1: I might, especially if the arrow keeps changing. That could make me bump into someone.

Facilitator: What could reduce the need to look down?

P1: Vibration when I am facing the correct direction or when I get closer.

Facilitator: Would you expect the system to show your friend’s exact location?

P1: The distance makes it feel quite exact. If it is only approximate, the interface should say that.

Observation: P1 understood the navigation metaphor but identified risks involving barriers, accuracy, distraction and implied precision.

### Predefined-message concept

Facilitator: Before attending the festival, users choose or customise messages in a phone application. Those messages are then stored on the watch. During the event, the user can select one without typing. What do you think of this concept?

P1: It makes sense because typing on a watch would be difficult. It would also be quicker.

Facilitator: What messages would you prepare?

P1: “Taking a break,” “Going to another stage,” “Meet at the entrance,” “Wait for me,” “I’m okay” and “Need help.”

Facilitator: Imagine this screen displays “Taking a break.” What do you expect to happen after you press OK?

P1: It sends the message to the group.

Facilitator: Does the screen clearly show who receives it?

P1: No. I assume the whole group, but it should say “Send to group” or let me choose a person.

Facilitator: What would you expect after pressing OK?

P1: A confirmation such as “Sent to four friends.”

Facilitator: Would “Taking a break” provide enough information?

P1: Maybe not. My friends might not know whether to follow me.

Facilitator: What would improve it?

P1: Maybe “Taking a break-I’m okay” or “Taking a break-join me.”

Facilitator: Would having many versions make the menu difficult?

P1: Yes. It should only include a few important options.

### Social coordination scenario

Facilitator: Imagine you send “Taking a break,” and your three friends receive it. What do you expect them to do?

P1: They might continue watching the performance. One person might check whether I am okay.

Facilitator: Does the message itself coordinate a group response?

P1: Not completely. It informs them, but they still have to decide what it means.

Facilitator: What could the watch add without creating a complicated conversation?

P1: A reply such as “OK,” “Coming with you” or “Meet later.”

Facilitator: Would that improve your awareness of the group?

P1: Yes. I would know they saw the message and understand the plan.

### SOS screen

Facilitator: This is the SOS screen. It says “Send SOS to group?” and “OK presses: 0/3.” What do you think you must do?

P1: Press OK three times to confirm. Back cancels it.

Facilitator: Why do you think the design requires three presses?

P1: To prevent an accidental SOS.

Facilitator: Would that work?

P1: Yes, but three presses could be slow in a real emergency.

Facilitator: What alternative would you consider?

P1: Press and hold for two seconds. But that might also be hard if someone is injured.

Facilitator: Who do you think receives the SOS?

P1: It says “to group,” so only my friends.

Facilitator: Would you assume festival security or emergency services receive it?

P1: No, but the final confirmation should still make that clear.

Facilitator: What information should the recipients receive?

P1: My name, that it is urgent and the option to find me. Maybe also the time it was sent.

Facilitator: Should the watch automatically share location after SOS?

P1: Probably yes, but only during the emergency. It should stop afterward.

Facilitator: Should there be a cancellation period?

P1: Yes, maybe five seconds in case I make a mistake.

### Privacy questions

Facilitator: How would you feel if group members could select your name and find you at any time during the festival?

P1: I would be okay with it during the event if I joined the group willingly. But I would want to know when someone is finding me.

Facilitator: Should users be able to refuse?

P1: Yes. There should be a privacy mode or a request that I accept.

Facilitator: Would requiring permission every time make the feature less useful?

P1: Maybe. The group could agree before the festival, but the watch should still notify me whenever someone starts finding me.

### Hardware preference

Facilitator: For the final prototype, the team is considering a round touch display and a round rotary touch display. Which sounds more suitable?

P1: I would like the rotary version for moving between friends and messages. It could be easier than tapping Next repeatedly.

Facilitator: What would touch still be useful for?

P1: Selecting a large option or confirming. A combination could be best.

Facilitator: What should we compare when testing the hardware?

P1: Whether the rotary control is easy while walking, whether it turns accidentally and whether touch works with sweaty hands.

### Closing questions

Facilitator: What is the strongest part of the concept?

P1: Finding friends without taking out a phone. The arrow and colour make the selected person clear.

Facilitator: What is the weakest or most uncertain part?

P1: Whether the location and distance will be accurate. Also, both people might keep moving.

Facilitator: Does the watch support cooperation?

P1: It could, but the current flow mainly gives one person information. It would be more cooperative if the friend could respond and agree to wait.

Facilitator: When would you not use it?

P1: If it tracked me continuously without telling me or if the direction was unreliable.

Facilitator: Is there anything else you would change?

P1: Show delivery confirmation and connection status for each friend.

Facilitator: Thank you. That concludes the session.

## Transcript 2

### Introduction and consent

Facilitator: Thank you for participating. I will show you a mid-fidelity interface prototype for a festival watch. The current prototype only demonstrates screens and proposed interactions. It does not send real messages, detect people, calculate directions or contact anyone through SOS.

I will ask you to explain what you think each screen means and what you would expect the final system to do. We are evaluating the design rather than you. Your participation is voluntary, and your name will not be recorded. Do you consent to participate?

P2: Yep.

Facilitator: Can you describe your experience with concerts or festivals?

P2: I have attended some concerts and one multi-stage festival. At the festival, my friends and I separated because we wanted to see different performers.

Facilitator: Did you experience any communication problems?

P2: Yeah. Messages took a long time to send, and calling was difficult because the music was loud.

### Initial interpretation

Facilitator: Please examine the home screen and tell me what you think this device is for.

P2: It looks like a watch for keeping a group connected. The text says “Together, further,” and it shows four connected people.

Facilitator: What functions do you expect?

P2: Finding friends, sending quick messages and SOS.

Facilitator: What does the Bluetooth symbol communicate?

P2: The watches are connected locally, so they may not need mobile reception.

Facilitator: Would you trust that assumption based only on the icon?

P2: Not completely. It should say “offline connection active” if that is what it means.

Facilitator: What information would reassure you before entering the festival?

P2: That every friend’s watch is connected and the prepared messages are saved.

### Menu navigation

Facilitator: What would you do to navigate this interface?

P2: Use Back and Next to move through options and OK to select.

Facilitator: Is that interaction suitable for a concert?

P2: It is simple, but repeatedly pressing Next could be annoying if there are many options.

Facilitator: What alternative might work?

P2: A rotary edge would be quicker. Turn it to browse and press it to select.

Facilitator: Would a touchscreen be useful?

P2: Yes, but the screen is small. Large touch targets would be necessary.

### Predefined messages

Facilitator: The messages are selected through a phone application before the event and saved to the watch. Why might this be useful?

P2: You do not have to type, and the messages should still be available if there is no internet.

Facilitator: What could go wrong?

P2: Someone might forget to prepare them. The application should include defaults.

Facilitator: What default messages would you expect?

P2: “I’m okay,” “Where are you?”, “Taking a break,” “Meet at our meeting point,” “Wait for me” and “Need help.”

Facilitator: The mock-up shows “Taking a break.” What does that mean to you as a recipient?

P2: My friend is leaving the crowd temporarily.

Facilitator: Would you know whether you should follow?

P2: No. I might be worried if they leave alone.

Facilitator: How could the system improve that?

P2: Add a second part, such as “I’m okay” or “Please come with me.”

Facilitator: What would you expect after the sender chooses the message?

P2: A screen asking whether to send it to one friend or the whole group.

Facilitator: What feedback should appear after sending?

P2: “Message sent” and then an acknowledgement when someone sees it.

Facilitator: The prototype cannot currently test sending or receipt. How could we test that later?

P2: Give two people physical watches and check whether the notification is noticeable in noise without becoming distracting.

### Find Friend menu

Facilitator: This screen displays Find Friend. What do you think happens if you select it?

P2: It opens the group-member list.

Facilitator: The next screen displays “Nayaka,” “Purple” and “OK to Find.” Is that clear?

P2: Mostly. Purple must be Nayaka’s group colour.

Facilitator: Would you need a photograph or icon?

P2: Maybe not for a small friend group. Name and colour should be enough.

Facilitator: What if two people have similar names or colours?

P2: The phone application should prevent duplicate colours or let people choose an icon.

Facilitator: What do you expect after pressing OK?

P2: I expect the direction screen to open and Nayaka to receive a notification.

Facilitator: Why should Nayaka receive a notification?

P2: Because someone is accessing information about where they are. They should know and decide whether to wait.

### Understanding the navigation display

Facilitator: Now look at the direction screen. Explain everything you see.

P2: It is finding Nayaka. Nayaka is shown as 24 metres away. The purple arrow points diagonally left, so I should move that way.

Facilitator: The final concept proposes an edge light working like a compass. Would you understand that?

P2: Not immediately. I do not normally use a compass.

Facilitator: Does the central arrow help?

P2: Yes. Without the arrow, I might not understand the edge light.

Facilitator: What instruction would you need?

P2: Something like “Turn until the arrow points up.” It could show that the watch should remain flat.

Facilitator: Do you think the arrow points directly to Nayaka or towards a route?

P2: Directly to Nayaka.

Facilitator: Could that create a problem?

P2: Yes. I might walk towards a barrier or through a crowded section because it looks like a route.

Facilitator: How should that be communicated?

P2: Say “Direction only-choose a safe path.”

Facilitator: What if Nayaka is moving?

P2: The arrow will keep changing. That could be frustrating.

Facilitator: How should the system respond?

P2: Ask Nayaka to stay still temporarily. Or both users could agree on a fixed meeting point instead.

Facilitator: What would happen when you became close?

P2: I would expect a vibration or a message saying “Nearby.” Then I would look around rather than keep watching the screen.

### Social coordination

Facilitator: Imagine Nayaka is watching a performance and you activate Find Friend. What should happen socially?

P2: Nayaka should receive a small notification saying I am trying to find them.

Facilitator: What responses should be available?

P2: “I’ll wait,” “I’m moving,” “Meet at checkpoint,” or “Not now.”

Facilitator: Why include “Not now”?

P2: People may intentionally separate. Being in a group should not mean anyone can demand that you reunite immediately.

Facilitator: Does the current interface communicate that autonomy?

P2: No. It looks like I can immediately start tracking the selected friend.

Facilitator: What change would you recommend?

P2: Show “Request direction” instead of “Find,” then wait for the other person to accept-unless the group enabled automatic sharing beforehand.

Observation: P2 interpreted the current wording as potentially enabling immediate tracking and recommended explicit consent or pre-agreed permissions.

### Potential distraction

Facilitator: How often do you think you would look at the watch while finding someone?

P2: Probably every few seconds.

Facilitator: What effect might that have?

P2: I would pay less attention to the people and obstacles around me. It could also make me miss part of the performance.

Facilitator: What could reduce that problem?

P2: Haptic instructions. For example, one vibration when I am facing correctly and faster vibration when I am close.

Facilitator: Could vibration create a different problem?

P2: Yes. At a loud concert, there may already be bass vibration. Users should choose the strength and pattern.

P2: It opens a confirmation screen, so it has not sent yet.

Facilitator: What do you need to do?

P2: Press OK three times. The counter changes from zero to three.

Facilitator: Is that clear?

P2: Yes.

Facilitator: Does it prevent accidents?

P2: Probably, although someone could still press quickly without reading.

Facilitator: Would you prefer three presses, holding the button or another gesture?

P2: Maybe hold SOS first, then press OK once. That uses two different actions.

Facilitator: What if the user has only one free hand or is injured?

P2: It needs to be possible with one hand. The buttons must also be large.

Facilitator: What do you expect after the third press?

P2: A strong vibration and “SOS sent to group.” My friends should receive my name and direction.

Facilitator: Would you assume security has been contacted?

P2: Some people might. It should clearly say “Friends notified-festival staff not automatically contacted.”

Facilitator: Should SOS override location privacy?

P2: Only temporarily, because the person has deliberately activated it.

### Trust and technical expectations

P2: Quite accurate, because it gives a specific number.

Facilitator: What if the hardware can only provide an approximate estimate?

P2: Use “about 25 metres” or ranges like “near” and “far.”

Facilitator: How would you react if the arrow changed frequently?

Facilitator: What should the system show if the signal is weak?

### Hardware comparison

Facilitator: What social value does it support?

## 4. Evaluation findings

### Finding 1: The overall concept was understandable

Both proxy users interpreted the device as a group communication and friend-finding watch for festivals.

They understood:

- The connected group indicator

- The Back, OK and Next controls

- The purpose of predefined messages

- The Find Friend selection sequence

- The SOS confirmation counter

However, their understanding reflects expectations created by the interface, not evidence that the functions work.

#### Design response

Retain the simple three-control navigation and clear separation between normal communication, friend finding and SOS.

### Finding 2: The offline communication model requires clearer explanation

Both users assumed the Bluetooth icon meant the watch would work without mobile internet. The prototype currently cannot demonstrate this.

Users wanted confirmation that:

- Messages had transferred from the phone

- Messages were stored on the watch

- Every friend’s watch was connected

- Individual members had not disconnected

- A message had been successfully sent

#### Design response

Future screens should include:

- “Offline messages ready”

- Individual connection status

- “Sent,” “delivered” and “acknowledged” states

- Honest feedback when delivery cannot be confirmed

Until the hardware is implemented, the team should describe offline communication as a proposed capability rather than a tested outcome.

### Finding 3: Predefined messages may support awareness but remain socially ambiguous

“Taking a break” tells the group what someone is doing but not what response is expected.

Users still need to interpret:

- Whether the person is safe

- Whether someone should accompany them

- Whether they are leaving temporarily

- Where they plan to go

- Whether the group should wait

#### Design response

Use short messages that combine status and intention:

- Taking a break - I’m okay

- Taking a break - join me

- Moving to another stage - continue without me

- Meet at our checkpoint

- Wait for me

- Need help

Keep the number of watch messages limited and allow users to select relevant defaults through the phone application.

### Finding 4: Message recipients and acknowledgement must be visible

The mock-up did not clearly communicate whether messages would be sent to one person or the entire group.

Both users expected:

- Recipient selection

- Confirmation before sending

- Delivery feedback

- Acknowledgement from friends

#### Design response

Show a short flow:

- Choose message.

- Choose “person” or “group.”

- Confirm sending.

- Show delivery state.

- Allow one-tap responses such as “OK,” “Coming” or “Meet later.”

### Finding 5: Find Friend currently represents individual information access rather than full cooperation

The interface allows one user to choose another and begin finding them. It does not currently show whether the selected friend knows, agrees or changes their behaviour.

Both users recognised that successful reconnection requires coordinated roles. If both people continue moving independently, directional information may become confusing.

#### Design response

Change the interaction into a reciprocal process:

- User selects a friend.

- The selected friend receives a request.

- The selected friend chooses “I’ll wait,” “Come to me,” “Meet at checkpoint” or “Not now.”

- Both watches display the agreed plan.

- Navigation ends when the users reunite or the session expires.

This would support cooperation rather than merely displaying another person’s location.

### Finding 6: The central arrow is more immediately understandable than the compass light

Both users understood the arrow. P2, who was less familiar with compass navigation, considered it necessary.

The edge light may still support peripheral awareness, but users may need an initial explanation.

#### Design response

- Retain the central arrow.

- Use the edge light as complementary feedback.

- Add “Turn until the arrow faces forward.”

- Provide optional haptic confirmation when facing the appropriate direction.

- Evaluate whether the physical rotary display provides an intuitive connection between turning the control and changing direction.

### Finding 7: The interface may imply greater accuracy than the system can provide

Displaying “24 m” creates an expectation of precise distance. A confident arrow may similarly imply reliable direction.

The current prototype cannot verify either.

#### Design response

If hardware testing reveals limited accuracy:

- Display “approximately 25 m”

- Use distance ranges such as far, medium, near and very near

- Show “direction uncertain” when signals are unstable

- Avoid presenting the arrow as a navigable route

- Include “Choose a safe path” during first use

### Finding 8: Watch-based navigation may create visual distraction

Both users anticipated looking repeatedly at the display while moving through a crowd.

This could:

- Reduce environmental awareness

- Increase collision risk

- Distract from the performance

- Encourage users to follow a direct line through unsafe spaces

#### Design response

The hardware prototype should test:

- Haptic confirmation of correct orientation

- Haptic proximity patterns

- Short glanceable prompts

- Screen timeout

- Stationary checking rather than continuous walking and viewing

Users should be told to stop and check the watch rather than continuously watching it while moving.

### Finding 9: SOS confirmation is understandable but requires further comparison

Both users understood the requirement to press OK three times. They believed it could reduce accidental activation, but they questioned whether it would be too slow or difficult during distress.

#### Design response

Compare:

- Three OK presses

- Press-and-hold

- SOS followed by one confirmation

- Rotary confirmation

- A short cancellation period after transmission

The interface must explicitly state:

- Who received the alert

- Whether festival staff were contacted

- Whether temporary location sharing started

- What recipients should do next

### Finding 10: Privacy and autonomy must be visible in the interaction

Participants were willing to share location with trusted friends during the event but did not want invisible or continuous tracking.

P2 also identified the possibility of social pressure when someone refuses to be found.

#### Design response

- Notify users when someone initiates Find Friend.

- Require consent or establish permissions before the event.

- Provide “Not now” and privacy mode.

- Limit friend-finding sessions by time.

- Stop sharing after reconnection.

- Avoid permanent location histories.

- Explain when SOS temporarily overrides normal privacy settings.

### Finding 11: The rotary display is promising but must be tested against touch

Participants expected rotary control to be useful for browsing messages and friends, while touch could support direct selection.

Neither option has yet been physically evaluated.

#### Hardware comparison criteria

Test both boards using identical tasks:

- Select a predefined message

- Choose a recipient

- Select a friend

- Cancel Find Friend

- Confirm SOS

- Complete tasks while standing

- Complete tasks while walking slowly

- Use with one hand

- Use with damp or sweaty fingers

Collect:

- Selection errors

- Accidental activation

- Completion time

- Number of times users look at the screen

- Perceived confidence

- Physical comfort

- Preference and explanation

## 5. Overall evaluation conclusion

The interface communicates the central concept successfully: friends prepare short messages before an event, access them through a watch and use approximate directional information to reconnect without relying heavily on phones.

However, this walkthrough also shows that displaying another person’s status or direction does not automatically produce cooperation. Users still need to understand the sender’s intention, acknowledge messages and coordinate who will move or wait.

The most important next iteration is therefore to develop reciprocal interactions:

- Message acknowledgement

- Clear recipient selection

- Find Friend requests

- Waiting or movement responses

- Consent-based temporary location sharing

- Clear endings to navigation sessions

Technical claims about offline communication, Bluetooth range, direction, distance, haptics and SOS delivery must remain labelled as proposed until evaluated through the physical ESP32-S3 prototypes.

## 6. Evaluation limitations

This evaluation cannot determine:

- Whether watches can communicate reliably in a crowded venue

- Whether the technology genuinely works without internet

- Whether compass direction is accurate

- Whether distance estimates are reliable

- Whether LEDs are visible outdoors

- Whether haptic signals can be distinguished from loud bass

- Whether messages reach every group member

- Whether the SOS alert is delivered

- Whether battery life is sufficient

- Whether rotary or touch interaction performs better physically
