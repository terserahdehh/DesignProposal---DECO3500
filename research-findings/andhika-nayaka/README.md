# Andhika Nayaka — Research Findings

Research conducted for the PulseBand concept: a wrist-worn experience intended
to help groups reconnect, exchange quick signals, and access useful venue cues
at concerts and music festivals.

## Headline insight

> Reliable coordination is a stronger user need than adding more entertainment
> features. Any haptic experience should support user choice rather than assume
> that all hard-of-hearing attendees want the same thing.

## Evidence summary

| Theme | Evidence | Opportunity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connection | Weak reception makes it difficult to locate separated friends or know whether they have moved. | Explore friend-finding that remains useful when normal mobile reception is unreliable. |
| Attention | Checking a phone repeatedly can interrupt the event experience. | Make essential group information glanceable on a wearable. |
| Loud environments | Calling, speaking, and getting someone's attention become unreliable near performances. | Use clear visual cues and optional haptic notifications. |
| Accessibility | Hearing needs vary, and amplified concert sound may already be intense. | Let users control vibration strength and disable it entirely. |
| Navigation | Venue maps can be confusing; signs often provide a simpler fallback. | Prefer simple directional cues over a dense map interface. |
| Adoption | The concept is most valuable during moments of separation, not necessarily throughout an event. | Design for fast, occasional interactions with minimal setup. |

## Supporting research

### Netnography

1. [Finding friends without cell service](https://www.reddit.com/r/aves/comments/11ewu0u/finding_people_at_raves_when_theres_no_cell/) — festival attendees describe difficulty finding friends when reception is poor and use pre-arranged meeting points as a fallback.
2. [Phone use while trying to find friends](https://www.reddit.com/r/aves/comments/1pglwa1/unpopular_opinion_i_hate_having_to_spend_so_much/) — attendees discuss how repeatedly checking a phone to coordinate can distract from the festival experience.

### Secondary research

[Touching the Audience: Musical Haptic Wearables for Live Music Performances](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00779-020-01395-2) explores wearable haptics in live music. It supports investigating vibration as an engagement channel while preserving user control over intensity and whether the feature is enabled.

## Design principles

1. **Connection first.** Prioritise finding friends and communicating essential intentions.
2. **Glance, act, return.** Keep every interaction short enough that attention returns quickly to the performance.
3. **Design for degraded connectivity.** The central use case occurs precisely when ordinary messaging is unreliable.
4. **Keep haptics adjustable.** Offer intensity controls and a clear off state.
5. **Do not generalise accessibility needs.** Treat hearing experiences and preferences as individual.
6. **Show direction simply.** A lightweight cue should be easier to interpret than a full venue map.

## Current concept priorities

- Colour-coded friend proximity or direction cues
- Quick, pre-written group signals such as “toilet,” “food,” or “taking a break”
- Custom message setup through a companion app
- Operation with little or no dependence on venue Wi-Fi or mobile reception
- Optional visual and haptic feedback

## Limitations

- Interview 4 in the combined PDF represents one person's experience and should not be treated as representative of all concertgoers or all hard-of-hearing people.
- The first usability session evaluated an early phone-based representation of a wearable, not a functional wrist-worn device.
- Technical feasibility and offline communication were discussed conceptually and were not validated in these sessions.
