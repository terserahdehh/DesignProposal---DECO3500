## Transcript

**Interviewer (Nayaka):** Hi, thank you for helping me with this interview. Since we already know each other, I wanted to ask you because I know you have experience going to concerts and music festivals. I think your perspective could be useful for our project, especially because you also have personal experience with hearing difficulties.

For DECO3500, my team is researching how technology could improve people's experiences at concerts and music festivals. We are exploring an idea for a wearable wristband. It could potentially help friends stay connected when a venue is crowded or phone reception is poor. We are also considering features such as lights and vibrations that respond to music. Because the idea is still developing, I mainly want to understand your actual experiences rather than simply ask whether you like our idea.

Before we start, is it okay if I record this conversation? The recording will only be used for our university project so that I can accurately review and analyse your answers afterwards.

**Participant:** Yes, that's okay. I consent to being recorded.

**Interviewer:** Thank you. If there is anything you do not want to answer, that is completely fine. First, can you tell me about the most recent concert or music festival you attended?

**Participant:** My last music festival was Pesta Rakyat 2025, where I saw Project Pop performing here in Brisbane. Before that, I also attended Jazz Goes to Campus back in my home country.

**Interviewer:** So, you have experienced festivals both here in Brisbane and back home. What did you enjoy most about attending them?

**Participant:** I enjoyed seeing some of my favourite singers and bands live. There is something different about hearing musicians perform their songs live. They are usually more energetic and more in tune with the mood of the audience.

**Interviewer:** I understand. It is different from listening through Spotify or watching online because you are actually there with the artist and the crowd. Was there anything that made the experience difficult or frustrating?

**Participant:** Concerts and festivals are usually very crowded, and you may not get a good spot, especially if you do not buy VIP tickets. At the two recent festivals I mentioned, I was either placed at the back or too cramped among other people.

Another frustrating thing is Wi-Fi or signal issues. It was not much of a problem at Pesta Rakyat, but it was an issue during Jazz Goes to Campus. Sometimes it was hard for me to reach my friends when we were separated.

**Interviewer:** So, for you, the crowd is not only about getting a poor view. It can also make it harder to move around and stay connected with your friends?

**Participant:** Yes, definitely.

**Interviewer:** Who do you normally go to concerts or festivals with, and how do you communicate during the event?

**Participant:** I usually go with my friends. We normally communicate through group chats or private chats, either on WhatsApp or LINE.

**Interviewer:** Do you usually make a plan before going in, such as deciding where to meet if someone gets separated, or do you mostly rely on messaging?

**Participant:** Usually, we rely on messaging. Sometimes, we also decide on a place to meet again, especially if we know we are going to separate.

**Interviewer:** Have you experienced being separated from your friends during an event?

**Participant:** Yes. During Jazz Goes to Campus, we separated into two groups because some of us preferred different artists.

We tried to text friends in the other group. If one person's phone had a connection problem, another person would try to contact them instead. Then, we would normally arrange to meet again at a certain spot.

There was another time when I entered the event area much later than my friends because I was stuck in traffic. I could not reach them to say that I had arrived because the signal was weak.

Eventually, I found them waiting near an entrance that I happened to pass. It could have been a very different situation if I had not walked past that entrance or had not seen them there.

**Interviewer:** In that case, finding them was partly luck. If you had entered through another entrance, you might have spent much longer trying to find each other.

**Participant:** Exactly.

**Interviewer:** When that happens and you cannot reach your friends, does it make you worried, or is it more frustrating?

**Participant:** I think it is both. It can be frustrating because you do not know exactly where they are. If you cannot contact them, you also do not know whether they are waiting for you somewhere or have moved somewhere else.

**Interviewer:** Of poor phone reception, low battery, loud music, and crowded spaces, which affect communication most for you?

**Participant:** Poor phone reception and crowded spaces are probably the two main issues for me.

Weak reception makes it harder to contact my friends and ask where they are. Crowded spaces also make it difficult to move around and physically find them.

Loud music can also make communication difficult, especially when I am close to the stage or the song is a louder type of music.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned that you are hard of hearing in one ear and use hearing technology that helps you hear through both ears. Does a concert environment affect how you communicate compared with normal situations?

**Participant:** Yes, I think so. In general, it is already normal for everyone to have difficulty hearing people around them during a loud concert. It can be slightly more difficult for me because I am hard of hearing in one ear.

The hearing technology helps me hear from both sides in normal situations, but concerts are very loud and crowded, so communication can still be difficult. If my friend is standing right beside me, especially on my better-hearing side, I can usually communicate with them more easily. If someone is trying to call my name from far away during a concert, I may not hear them.

**Interviewer:** Even though the technology helps you hear from both sides, the concert environment creates another layer of difficulty.

**Participant:** Yes, especially because there are so many sounds happening at the same time.

**Interviewer:** So, even if your friend is physically close, calling your name might not always be an effective way to get your attention?

**Participant:** Yes.

**Interviewer:** What about low battery? Has that ever become a major problem?

**Participant:** Not really. Low battery is usually not an issue because I normally bring a power bank, and my battery health is still good.

**Interviewer:** It sounds like network connection is more important to you than battery. During the performance, how often do you use your phone?

**Participant:** Quite often. I normally use my phone to record the performance.

**Interviewer:** Some people say that using phones during concerts distracts them from being present. Do you feel that way?

**Participant:** Not really. I do not find it distracting because I use it to capture memories from the event.

**Interviewer:** So, phone use itself is not necessarily a problem for you. The problem is that the connection might not work when you need the phone for communication.

**Participant:** Yes, exactly.

**Interviewer:** I also wanted to ask about one part of our wearable idea. We are exploring whether music could be represented through vibration—for example, allowing someone to feel parts of the rhythm or bass through the wristband. How would you feel about experiencing music in that way?

**Participant:** I am unsure because I have never experienced musical elements solely through vibration, so I cannot imagine what it would feel like.

Concerts are normally loud enough for me to hear and enjoy. Even though I am hard of hearing in one ear, the music at concerts is usually loud enough that I can still experience it.

Most of the time, I do not need to use my hearing technology during concerts because the music can become too loud when I use it.

**Interviewer:** That is useful for us because we should not assume that someone automatically needs or wants vibration to experience music just because they are hard of hearing.

**Participant:** Yes, I think it depends on the person.

**Interviewer:** Perhaps vibration could be an additional option rather than something every hard-of-hearing user is expected to use?

**Participant:** Yes, I think that would be better.

**Interviewer:** If vibration were available as an optional feature, could continuous vibration become distracting or uncomfortable?

**Participant:** Yes, it could become distracting or uncomfortable. If you are using vibrations, users should be able to adjust them like a volume control on a device.

**Interviewer:** Rather than only switching vibration on or off, you would want control over how strong it is?

**Participant:** Yes. People should be able to adjust it based on what feels comfortable for them.

**Interviewer:** And turn it off completely if they do not want it?

**Participant:** Yes.

**Interviewer:** Another area we are looking at is finding information around the venue. Have you had difficulty finding seating, toilets, exits, quiet spaces, accessible areas, or other event information?

**Participant:** Yes, especially when the map provided by the organiser is confusing. Often, there are signs that help guide me to the right place.

**Interviewer:** Would you normally look at the map on your phone first, or mostly follow the physical signs around the venue?

**Participant:** It depends. If I already have the map, I might look at it. If the map is confusing, the physical signs are usually more useful.

**Interviewer:** Any navigation technology would need to be simpler than showing another complicated map.

**Participant:** Yes, I think so.

**Interviewer:** If you needed a break, wanted help, or wanted to separate from your friends for a while, how would you normally let them know?

**Participant:** I tell them directly if they are with me, or by text if they are not.

**Interviewer:** If the signal were poor at that moment, would that create the same communication problem we discussed earlier?

**Participant:** Yes. If they are not with me and I cannot send the message properly, it would be difficult for them to know what I am doing.

**Interviewer:** Last question: based on everything you have experienced, if you could improve one thing about concerts or music festivals, what would you change?

**Participant:** I would improve signal or reception at the concert or festival area so that it is easier to connect with others or access the information we need.

**Interviewer:** Staying connected would be a bigger priority for you than adding many new entertainment features?

**Participant:** Yes. Being able to contact people and access information reliably would be useful.

**Interviewer:** That is helpful. One important thing I have learned is that being hard of hearing does not mean everyone experiences concerts in the same way. In your case, you are hard of hearing in one ear and use hearing technology that helps you hear through both ears, but you may not want to use it at concerts because the sound is already very loud.

For our project, vibration should not be something we assume every hard-of-hearing attendee needs. It should probably be optional and adjustable. At the same time, difficulty communicating and finding friends when reception is poor seems like a much clearer problem from your experience.

**Participant:** Yes, I agree.

**Interviewer:** Thank you. This was useful for our research and for deciding which parts of the concept to focus on. Thank you again for letting me record the interview.

**Participant:** No worries. Good luck.

## Interview conclusion

The participant's highest-priority issue is reliable communication when mobile
reception is poor. Crowds and loud sound compound this problem by making it hard
to move, visually locate friends, or call for their attention. The findings
support a low-attention friend-finding tool, while showing that music haptics
must be optional and individually adjustable rather than framed as a universal
accessibility solution.
